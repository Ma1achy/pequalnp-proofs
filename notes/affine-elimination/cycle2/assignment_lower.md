# Matching and Hamiltonian-cycle lower bounds at an edge cut

Let the input describe a subgraph of the complete bipartite graph with $m$ rows and $m$ columns, or a subgraph of the complete directed graph on $m\ge2$ vertices. In the second case a solution is a single directed Hamiltonian cycle. For either problem, fix **any** static order of edge variables and split it at the midpoint. In the directed case count the off-diagonal edges. Extra loop variables can be fixed and ignored. For all sufficiently large $m$, every accepting rectangle cover across this cut has size at least $c2^m/\sqrt m$, with an absolute $c>0$. This applies both to deciding whether the indicated edges form a solution and to deciding whether they contain a solution. The constant can differ between problems.

This lower bound applies to any exact layered nondeterministic computation that uses this visible edge order once, even with auxiliary nondeterministic layers interleaved anywhere. A state at the cut gives the rectangle of prefixes that can reach it and suffixes that can continue from it to acceptance. Paths on the two sides can concatenate exactly when their cut states agree. Extra guessed variables used on both sides must be conveyed by the state for this model to apply. A width of at most $2^r$ at the cut therefore requires $r\ge m-\frac12\log_2m-O(1)$. If the state is a syndrome ranging over a rank-$r$ binary space, this bounds that rank. The statement counts all cut states, so any extra state apart from the syndrome must also be charged.

## Signatures and a rectangle fooling set

Color the first part of the edge order red and the second blue. A pure solution is the set of exactly $m$ edges of a permutation $\pi$. Its red edges match a set of rows $R$ bijectively to a set of columns $C$. Call $(R,C)$ its signature and write $z=|R|=|C|$. Keep one pure solution per attainable signature. Its pair of red and blue restrictions is an accepting input.

For two different signatures, at least one of the two crossed inputs fails. Indeed, the two crossed edge sets together contain exactly $2m$ edge occurrences. If each contains a solution, each must already consist of exactly $m$ edges forming a solution. Then in the first cross the red-row and red-column sets of the first signature must respectively complement the blue-row and blue-column sets of the second. This forces the signatures to agree. An accepting rectangle can contain at most one of the chosen pairs. For perfect matching the number of attainable signatures is also an upper bound on the rectangle count. For the contains-a-matching predicate the rectangle for $(R,C)$ requires that the red graph contain a bijection $R\to C$ and the blue graph contain a bijection of the complements. For exact incidence require the two graphs to consist precisely of those partial matchings. These rectangles cover every accepted input. For Hamiltonicity the signature only supplies a lower bound because the two pieces also have to form one cycle.

For minimum matching or tour cost one obtains the same obstruction to a tropical factorization $f(p,s)=\min_{j\le D}(a_j(p)+b_j(s))$, even allowing signed tables. Give selected edges cost zero and all other edges cost one. If the same summand attains zero on two diagonal pairs, its two crossed values also sum to zero. Every summand value is at least the corresponding nonnegative optimum, so both crossed inputs would have value zero, contradicting the fooling set. This uses only two costs but assumes a cut factorization. The general circuit result below has different hypotheses.

## Counting at a balanced cut

For perfect matching choose a uniform permutation on $[m]$ and let $Z$ count its red edges. At any fixed signature of size $z$ at most $z!(m-z)!$ permutations occur. If $D$ counts signatures, dividing by $m!$ and summing per size gives

$$D\ \ge\ \mathbb{E}\binom m Z.$$

For the directed case choose a uniform single-cycle permutation and consider a fixed signature with $0<z<m$. Put $t=|R\setminus C|$. The number of single-cycle permutations satisfying $\pi(R)=C$ equals

$$t\,(z-1)!\,(m-z-1)!.$$

If $t=0$, the nonempty proper set $R=C$ is invariant. If $t>0$, the red edges of such a cycle form $t$ paths from $R\setminus C$ to $C\setminus R$ with internal vertices $R\cap C$. For any fixed bijection between these endpoint sets, ordering the internal vertices and distributing them among the $t$ labeled paths gives $(z-t)!\binom{z-1}{t-1}=(z-1)!/(t-1)!$ fillings. The complementary blue paths have $(m-z-1)!/(t-1)!$ fillings per endpoint bijection. Choose a red endpoint bijection in $t!$ ways. There are $(t-1)!$ choices for the blue bijection making the composite a single cycle. Multiplication gives the formula. The edge coloring can only decrease this count. Since $t\le\min(z,m-z)$ and there are $(m-1)!$ cycles, each such signature has probability at most $2/\binom mz$. For $z=0,m$ that bound is immediate. Hence for directed cycles $D\ge\frac12\mathbb{E}\binom mZ$.

For perfect matching write $a_{ij}=1$ on red edges and $0$ otherwise. A bar indexed by a row, a column or neither denotes the respective mean. The matrix $q_{ij}=a_{ij}-\bar a_{i\bullet}-\bar a_{\bullet j}+\bar a$ has zero row and column sums. The centered red count equals $\sum_i q_{i,\pi(i)}$. The diagonal terms in its second moment contribute $\sum q_{ij}^2/m$. Pairwise sampling without replacement and the zero row and column sums give $\sum q_{ij}^2/[m(m-1)]$ from ordered distinct row pairs. Thus

$$\operatorname{Var} Z=\frac{\sum q_{ij}^2}{m-1}\le\frac{\sum(a_{ij}-\bar a)^2}{m-1}\le\frac{m^2}{4(m-1)}.$$

The first inequality follows from orthogonality to matrices of row and column effects. The balanced cut puts $\mathbb E Z=\lfloor m^2/2\rfloor/m$ within $1/(2m)$ of $m/2$.

For directed cycles the balanced off-diagonal cut has exactly $m(m-1)/2$ red arcs. Each appears with probability $1/(m-1)$, so $\mathbb EZ=m/2$. Generate the random cycle by drawing a uniform cyclic ordering from all $m!$ arrangements of the vertex labels. Expose labels in sequence. Swapping two still-available labels couples any two choices for the next entry and changes at most four directed adjacency indicators in the sum. Each Doob martingale increment has absolute value at most four. Orthogonality of the increments gives $\operatorname{Var}Z\le16m$.

Chebyshev gives probability at least $3/4$ that $|Z-m/2|\le 8\sqrt m+1$ for both problems. For fixed $K$, Stirling's factorial bounds give $\binom mz\ge c_K2^m/\sqrt m$ uniformly for integers $|z-m/2|\le K\sqrt m+1$ as $m\to\infty$. For example, in this range the binomial entropy exponent differs from $m\log 2$ by $O_K(1)$ and its factorial prefactor is $\Theta(m^{-1/2})$. This proves the midpoint bound. For the order listing whole rows, after $s$ complete rows the matching signatures are exactly the $\binom ms$ possible used-column sets; the row cut at $s=\lfloor m/2\rfloor$ attains that order of growth.

A simpler counting argument avoids concentration at the cost of a polynomial factor. For each pure solution, record the first cut immediately following its $s=\lfloor m/2\rfloor$th selected edge. Some cut is shared by at least $m!/m^2$ permutations or $(m-1)!/m^2$ cycles. Each matching signature there covers at most $s!(m-s)!$ solutions. The resulting respective lower bounds $\binom ms/m^2$ and $\binom ms/m^3$ already give $r\ge m-O(\log m)$ for a cut.

Briquel, Koiran and Meer already use the moving-cut pigeonhole argument and the per-rectangle factorial bound for permutation matrices. Their result gives linear primal pathwidth for existential CNF representations of the exact permutation relation. The static midpoint argument here counts every red size in a single cut and treats directed single-cycle permutations explicitly. No priority is asserted for the sharp midpoint estimate. [Briquel, Koiran and Meer, Lemmas 2–3 and permanent theorem](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dam.2010.09.007) · [Author manuscript](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/pascal.koiran/Publis/briquel-koiran-meer.pdf)

## Primary-source gate counts

Jerrum and Snir count binary operation gates in straight-line DAGs. Projection functions and *all fixed semiring scalars* are allowed inputs. For the two min-plus semirings the scalars and variable costs range over all reals with $+\infty$, or all nonnegative reals with $+\infty$. Semiring multiplication is ordinary addition of lengths. Their values below count those addition gates, allowing arbitrary numbers of min gates. A free scalar is independent of the edge input. A weight read from the instance is a variable leaf; branching or input-dependent precomputation of constants falls outside this circuit model. [Jerrum and Snir, §§2.1–2.3, pp. 876–878](https://snir.cs.illinois.edu/listed/J7.pdf#page=3)

- The exact number for a minimum-weight perfect matching in $K_{m,m}$ is $m(2^{m-1}-1)$ for $m\ge1$. For $m=1$, the only edge is already the answer. [§4.3, pp. 887–889](https://snir.cs.illinois.edu/listed/J7.pdf#page=14)
- For the minimum-weight **directed** Hamiltonian cycle on $m\ge3$ vertices, the exact number is $(m-1)((m-2)2^{m-3}+1)$. The two-vertex case is one addition of the two directed arcs. Under the cyclic-permutation convention the one-vertex case is its sole self-loop, requiring no addition. “4.4” is the section number. The paper gives matching upper bounds via paths rooted at one vertex. [§4.4, pp. 889–891](https://snir.cs.illinois.edu/listed/J7.pdf#page=16)

Their argument distinguishes formal Boolean-coefficient polynomials (sets of monomials) from evaluating a Boolean function. The straight-line min-plus gate bound alone makes no claim about an arbitrary SAT or matching decision program. They explicitly contrast polynomial-time branching assignment algorithms with the straight-line bound. [§§2.2–2.3, 5.2](https://snir.cs.illinois.edu/listed/J7.pdf)

## A three-cost transfer to the classical weighted bound

Let $\mathcal F$ be any finite nonempty family of $k$-element subsets of the input coordinates, $k\ge1$, and $f(w)=\min_{M\in\mathcal F}\sum_{e\in M}w_e$. A fixed binary min-plus circuit correct on **just** $\{0,1,k+1\}^E$ can be transformed without increasing the number of gates of either kind into a circuit correct on all nonnegative real weights and infinity. Arbitrary fixed finite signed scalar leaves and $+\infty$ are allowed. The argument does not rely on how many parse terms an expanded circuit would have. Expansion is used to prove the statement, and the gate transformation acts on the DAG.

1. Every finite output term has the form $c+u\cdot w$ with $u$ a vector of nonnegative integers. The zero input gives output zero, hence every finite intercept $c$ is nonnegative. Every term of intercept zero has a member of $\mathcal F$ in its support: set the support costs to zero and every outside cost to $k+1$. Otherwise the target would be at least $k+1$ while the circuit gives zero.
2. Fix $M\in\mathcal F$. Use costs one on $M$, $k+1$ outside it. The optimum is $k$. A minimizing term has $c\le k$ and uses only $M$. If its support $S$ is a proper subset of $M$, costs zero on $S$ and $k+1$ outside would give a circuit value at most $c\le k$, below the true minimum of at least $k+1$. Thus every coordinate of $M$ occurs. Since the term costs exactly $k$ on this input, all its multiplicities are one and $c=0$.
3. The zero-intercept terms consequently include each member of $\mathcal F$ and dominate a member on nonnegative weights term by term. Their minimum equals $f$. To extract them, annotate each gate with its value at the all-zero input. A subcircuit whose annotation is infinity is identically infinity. Replace finite scalar leaves by zero. At a min gate retain only children with the least annotation, keeping one min gate for a tie. At a plus gate keep both children. Induction shows the resulting finite gates represent precisely the minimum-intercept terms of their original gates with the common intercept removed. The output originally had minimum intercept zero. This also evaluates correctly for infinite costs by the same support argument.

For matching and tour take $k=m$. The Jerrum–Snir lower bounds therefore already apply to exact circuits on these three integer costs of $O(\log m)$ bits each. The binary zero-level fooling-set proof above and the three-cost transfer prove different statements, since the former assumes a rectangular cut and the latter allows arbitrary min-plus DAGs. No novelty claim for these reformulations is made.

## Reproduction

`python3 assignment_lower.py` checks the single-cycle signature count on every pair of row and column subsets of equal nontrivial size for $2\le m\le7$. For both problems it enumerates every balanced edge split through $m=4$ and tests the probability-to-signature bound. For bipartite matching it checks the exact variance formula using rational arithmetic. Through $m=3$ it independently tests the crosses of one representative per signature against all feasible matchings or cycles.

`InterfaceCover.lean` gives the generic cut proof. It constructs the cover list of length $2^r$, regroups hidden assignments using surjectivity onto the two sides, and uses a fooling family of size $d$ to prove $d\le 2^r$. Instantiating the geometric syndrome state and constructing the graph signatures are mathematical arguments above and in the companion derivation. The generic lemma passed Lean 4.36.0 with the standard three axioms. Run `lean InterfaceCover.lean` in this directory.
